Analysis

Interpretations of Day Two Prioritization Results

  • The largest number of delegates (31) prioritized increased funding. 84% of those who placed dots approved of increased funding. This is notable because the recommendation did not move to the second round of voting due to opposition from 14% of delegates, preventing it from being prioritized for deeper analysis over the summer. However, the concerns associated with red dots were about where funding was directed and accountability for the use of funds, both issues that can be addressed with stricter oversight at the state or local levels.
  • Something happened with the “Individualized Pathways” recommendation from dot prioritizing to hand voting. While it seemed acceptable to nearly 100% of delegates in the first round, it only reached 53% support in the second round. It was indeed the second lowest ranked for “first priority” in the first round, but it also had the fewest red dots and the highest level of overall acceptance at 96%. Were people concerned that it may have been prioritized over other more important things, reflecting a problem with the voting process itself? Or when hearing only the high-level description were people confused about its purpose and goals? This remains an open question.
  • The lowest number of first place ratings was for “PK-8 Benchmarks and Curriculum,” with professional development and access to recommended curriculum, but it still received 80% of dot placers to say they could accept it. Opposition by red dot placers was not in principle but in practice, with a desire to ensure any priority item would benefit all students and concerns that this kind of support may already be in place. To note is the general desire in Maine for students to be achieving at higher levels on NAEP and in general in reading and math. This proposal was crafted to respond to that desire, so why was it so unpopular?
  • The priority with the lowest level of acceptance in phase one of prioritization, “Access to High-Quality Curriculum and Materials,” still gained 72% overall acceptance. Opposition, again, was largely not in principle but more in practice, with few noting that it may not benefit all students, and more focused on the potential need for additional policies to accompany it to have real impact and the potential extra word it may cause teachers unless additional support was included. Some delegates did have a concern about this option reducing local control, however, the proposal did not indicate any reduction in local control. It proposed MDOE would provide access and support. Specifically, no accountability measures were included. Was this a case of mistaken understanding, or was something left unspoken?
  • Overall, there was a relatively high level of support for each of the nine proposals with concerns tending toward equitability (will the proposal impact all students or just some or be necessary in all districts or just some) and a desire not to reduce “local control.”

Definitions and Useful Information

A short glossary of terms used throughout this packet. The general definitions clarify concepts that recur across the recommendations; the analysis-specific definitions explain the five scales used to profile each policy option in the briefs below.

General useful information and definitions

Life Ready
The core skill set needed for adult life is truly interdisciplinary and the core skill set has impact across multiple life domains. We define “life ready” as more than the sum of these parts: fundamental academic skills + durable human skills + self-knowledge and futures planning skills + careers knowledge + adulting skills. With these skills in place, young people can confidently step into the adult world ready to choose a path and traverse it successfully.
Experiential Education
“Experiential education is a teaching philosophy that informs many methodologies in which educators purposefully engage with learners in direct experience and focused reflection in order to increase knowledge, develop skills, clarify values, and develop people’s capacity to contribute to their communities.”1
Local Control
Locally elected citizens make school district policy and govern public schools, acting in compliance with state statutes.2
Accountability
The MDOE implements a system of measuring school performance against specific indicators that align with federal requirements and providing three levels of support to schools in response. That is state accountability.3 The current MDOE is also implementing a new initiative called Measure What Matters, which assesses schools against a broader set of criteria that more fully reflect what Maine people value. This is entirely separate from the federally-mandated accountability plans. Local accountability is the idea that local communities can design their own metrics against which they can assess school or student performance and then update their district’s educational plan in response in order to support student and school progress. The Measure What Matters framework can be adopted by local districts voluntarily.4

Analysis-specific definitions

Reach
The breadth of impact of the policy across groups.
Implementation
The amount of work of bringing the policy into reality on the ground.
Political Will
The level of commitment from politicians needed for policy success.
Impact
The pace, depth, and character of change the policy is striving to achieve. Incremental is slow and accumulative. Transformative is faster and more fundamental.
Mechanism
Whether the policy uses incentives (carrots) or requirements/mandates (sticks) to achieve its goals.

The recommendations

Policy Briefs - Part One

Delegates' recommended topic areas that did not gain two-thirds support are included in Part One. Each topic is addressed in a brief summary analysis below. Some topics have multiple options; options were chosen to address each stated problem in ways determined to be broadly popular and also likely to be effective based on prior knowledge and feedback. (Note that these topics did not receive as robust a treatment as those topics that gained more than two-thirds support on Day Two. More detailed work will follow for any topic that is prioritized by two-thirds or more of delegates on Day Three.)

Topic area  ·  More Voice in Schools

Voice in Education Policy Making

Design and implement accessible and impactful ways for students, teachers, parents, and community members to understand and influence local and state-level education policy making.

Problem

The education system and schools themselves are too often not responsive enough to student, teacher, and community needs. Responsiveness is understood not to be a lack of interest by leaders in meeting needs, but an absence of effective mechanisms for seeking the appropriate engagement of the full community. The problem can lead to disconnected and disaffected students, siloed teachers, frustrated parents, and community members who vote no on school budgets—a recipe for low levels of support for public schools. For example, many agree that school board meetings do not necessarily provide adequate opportunities for community, student, and parent education on school governance nor opportunities for their voice to influence policy.

Option 1

State Legislation to Support a Biannual Citizens’ Assembly on Education

This policy option would create a state statute to enable a citizens’ assembly on education every two years, roughly following the phases of this assembly but on a quicker timeline. The first year would include broad listening through small-group conversations across Maine to reflect on the state of the system and set the agenda for the upcoming assembly. An in-person assembly would be held the second year, organized by an entity independent of the Maine Department of Education. Evaluations and continuous improvement plans would ensure citizen, legislator, and stakeholder organization feedback shapes each subsequent assembly.

Considerations

Future assemblies could be directed by legislation to have their own steering committee comprising specific stakeholder groups, to be organized by specific entities other than USM/CEPARE, or to provide specific types of recommendations on a timeline conducive to legislative action. Future assemblies would be effective to the extent that learnings from the evaluation are implemented, a broadly representative subset of the public is supported to participate, and elected leadership commits to listening and working with the results in an effective bipartisan manner over time.

Cost implications

Variable, and modest in the larger education budget. The staff costs for an organization such as CEPARE to plan and convene an event are relatively modest, and efficiencies would accrue with repetition. The costs for citizen participation depend on the duration, location, and number of participants. The budget for this hybrid in-person assembly, its external evaluation, and reporting to the legislature is $240,000. Virtual convenings are vastly less expensive. Estimated cost for the broad listening in year one would be $75,000. Philanthropy could be invited to participate to contribute specifically to building Maine digital infrastructure for bringing Maine citizens’ voices to decision makers in a variety of ways which expand the impact of citizen assemblies.

Stakeholder input

Stakeholder input via surveys regarding this assembly and anecdotal feedback from stakeholder organizations and legislators indicates likely support for this option.

Model Policies for Increasing Local Voice in School Board Decisions

This policy option would use state statute to direct MDOE, in collaboration with the Maine School Board Association (MSBA), to design and share model policies and strategies for local districts to encourage meaningful student, teacher, parent, and community engagement in decision making through effective approaches and tools that bring a diversity of voices to moderated discussions.

Considerations

There is precedent for using this mechanism to encourage local districts to take action of some sort. The resources needed are minimal, however, the policy would impact MDOE and MSSA so gaining their support for such legislation would be essential. These need to be convened by a neutral party on neutral ground, not by school board for school board. Professional moderation is likely necessary.

The cost of developing a model policy is modest; it could be led by MDOE professionals (at the cost of their salaried time) or an outside consultant. The cost of implementing the recommended practices at the local level would depend on many factors, including the strategies selected and the possible need for additional district staff to lead the work.

Maine Education Association, Maine Principals Association, and Maine School Management Association (which includes the Maine School Boards Association and the Maine Superintendents Association) participated in this assembly, indicating general support for more voice in education.

Plans should be searchable by statutorily-required element (e.g. curriculum alignment with learning results, multi-tiered system of support, provision of family outreach and engagement) so that citizens can compare their local policies to other districts.

Topic area  ·  Invest in Early Childhood Workforce

Early Childhood Workforce

A stronger, more consistent workforce will result in better early childhood care and education, resulting in better-prepared school children and fewer learning and behavior challenges.

Problem

Early childhood care and education has persistent and impactful workforce gaps. While the state currently funds salary supplements on a tiered basis, worker shortages remain, particularly in some geographic areas.

Option 1

Increased Funding for Salary Supplements for Early Childhood Care and Education Providers

This policy option would further invest in the workforce by providing increased salary supplements for certified educators and licensed home care providers in certain underserved areas. If a worker is eligible for supplements under the current program and works in a geographic area identified by DHHS as having a shortage of quality child care they would qualify for an increased monthly amount.

Considerations

Salary supplements have been in place since 2023. A recent evaluation report shows they are achieving early positive impacts on worker retention and satisfaction, though some areas still report shortages (a recruitment issue). Health care was also identified by 90% of survey respondents as a big unmet need for this workforce.

Cost implications

The total cost of providing additional salary supplements would depend on the amount of the increase as well as the specific criteria used by DHHS to identify worker shortage areas. The current program costs about $30M per year and supports over 7,000 workers with stipends ranging from $240 to $540 per month depending on qualifications.

Stakeholder input

We heard that teachers themselves also need early childhood care. A more stable early childhood workforce is “public good” and could substantially support a stronger PK-12 workforce overall.

Option 2

Regional Shared Learning Opportunities for Parents, Caregivers, and Educators

This policy option would invest in all adults who provide learning opportunities for young children, serving parents alongside paid professionals. Learning opportunities would be provided regionally to ensure local relevance and efficient use of resources. Each regional hub would serve to connect adult learners to existing partners including DHHS, Child Development Services, local school departments, and non-profits.

Considerations

While we don’t traditionally consider those who perform labor at home to be part of the workforce, parents working as full-time care and early educators at home are providing the same benefits to society as professionals when they are well-informed and connected to appropriate resources. Parents who take advantage of these learning opportunities may also choose to open home care centers and thereby serve other children and families. Experience gained in the home setting is excellent training for future roles in the paid workforce; as we have sought to boost educational technicians into classroom teacher positions, we can do the same for parents.

Bridging CDS, DHHS, and education is a significant effort with the potential for meaningful impact. The current divides create barriers for parents and caregivers and ultimately negatively impact children. In particular, there is a need for sustained efforts to identify students with special education needs as early as possible, which requires cross-system partnership.

Additionally, the regional hub concept is gaining interest across the state as a response to a variety of increasing needs that are difficult to meet at the local level.

Cost implications

A recent legislative attempt with many similar features (LD 1859, An Act to Improve Access to Child Care and Early Childhood Education by Establishing Regional Resource Hubs) estimated costs of creating four regional hubs at $750,000 per year, plus the cost of one coordinator position at DHHS. The four hubs that would have been established would have fostered “coordination, collaboration and planning related to early childhood programs and family support programs in order to provide resources to parents, educators, employers and community members.”

The bill was initially passed by legislators but ultimately failed because it was not selected by the Appropriations Committee for funding.

Stakeholder input

Stakeholders were not specifically asked about this idea. The idea of regional service hubs has been well-received in other contexts as an efficient and low-barrier strategy for facilitating coordination and knowledge-sharing.

Topic area  ·  Increase Funding

State Funding of Education

Ensure state and local funding combine to cover provision of the essentials of a high quality Maine education for all students no matter where they live.

Problem

Fairness is important to Maine people but not yet adequately reflected in how we fund Maine schools. Too many districts do not have sufficient resources to meet individual students’ educational needs and aspirations. Geography and local property wealth, in particular, are limiting factors, reducing or expanding important opportunities for students to learn, grow, and contribute.

Option 1

Targeted Additional Funding and Oversight Mechanisms

This policy option is a mechanism for directing to specific districts additional targeted funding for specific evidence-based or promising items or services. Districts could choose to provide the identified item or service and receive funding. For example, the state could provide funding to districts with higher levels of community poverty who provide yearlong high-quality afterschool programming and “late bus” transportation to all students, both of which have evidence of success in this setting. Funds and participation would be tracked separately for reporting and oversight to ensure effective and efficient use of funds.

Considerations

MEPRI has been tasked by the Education and Cultural Affairs Committee to assess the EPS formula for adequacy in covering the essentials of a Maine Education and to recommend changes as necessary by January 2027. The resulting report could be useful in assessing whether EPS needs broader changes or if a targeted funding approach is best.

Because EPS does not include accountability for how or where state funds directed to districts are spent, an additional layer of oversight is added in this option to meet fiscal responsibility and fairness concerns. Oversight for how some state funds are spent locally can be a cost effectiveness mechanism, especially when the desired outcome is greater fairness; more money does not necessarily equal better outcomes, but there are specific approaches and services that have been shown to provide a strong return on investment when provided to under-resourced students and communities.

Cost implications

Variable. Total cost depends on the intensity of services targeted for funding, level of state funding, and the number of districts / students to be served. Oversight measures should be tied as much as possible to other existing reporting mechanisms in order to reduce added costs at the local and state levels. Districts that have the most need for support often lack administrative capacity to manage applications, data collection and reporting; they may require additional support to access and use the targeted funds.

MEPRI recently studied a proposal to increase funding provided in the EPS model for economically disadvantaged students, which would have required an additional $19M in state funding. The formula currently allocates about $136M to support programs and services for economically disadvantaged students, but most of the funding is not targeted.

Stakeholder input

While many would be against the idea of across-the-board increases for education funding, there is support for focused spending initiatives that would be particularly beneficial for disadvantaged youth.

Option 2

Exploration of Sustainable Taxation Methods for Funding Essential Education Needs

This policy option would convene a cross-committee group of legislators to examine the two issues together: what is essential in a Maine education and what are sustainable ways to fund those essentials? The challenge of funding education and school-based services for children is shared by the legislative committees for education and health and human services as well as taxation, and should be examined collaboratively from all three perspectives.

Topic area  ·  PK-8 Benchmarks + Curriculum

PK-8 Academic Preparation for High School

Ensure all elementary and middle level students have access to high-quality learning experiences and robust academic supports to maximize the chances of arriving at high school meeting grade-level academic expectations.

Problem

Too many students arriving unprepared for the rigors of high school work.

Option 1

Access to Low-Cost High-Quality Curricula for K-4 Reading and Math

This policy option would invest MDOE staff time and expertise to pre-screen available curricula to recommend for schools to adopt. As a further step, MDOE could pursue state-level contracting for curricula through a competitive bidding process (similar to its technology procurement system), thus lowering the per-pupil costs.

Reach

Implementation

Light liftHeavy lift

Political will

MinimalSubstantial

Impact

IncrementalTransformative

Mechanism

CarrotStick

Considerations

Administrative turnover often leads to curricula and program changes as administrators seek to make their mark in a new district. However, when a new plan is rolled out every three to four years, this can impede progress. Having vetted low-cost, effective curricula use broadly around the state could disincentivize administrators from making quick changes. The criteria for recommended curricula could include it being at least in part experiential.

MDOE already provides guidance on criteria for high-quality curricula, and has the infrastructure for conducting group procurement. Stakeholder input

Teachers generally shared that the best curricula is neither proficiency-based nor reliant on strict pacing guides. Access to curricula that are vetted for quality and alignment with Maine Learning Results would be very helpful resources.

Option 2

Targeted Funding for Staff to Provide Learning Interventions

This policy option would provide state funding specifically for additional staff tasked with providing evidence-based academic interventions to students not meeting benchmarks within their grade year. The funding would be allocated to each district from the state share of education costs based on the proportion of their attending students that are performing in the bottom tier on state assessments.

Considerations

Schools are required to have MTSS systems in place as a basic expectation for school approval, and the highest (usually third) tier of interventions requires specialized training. Tier 3 specialists are an ongoing need, not a one-time investment; it is estimated that 1% to 5% of students may need this level of support. Total state cost estimate is about $30M; districts that already employ such specialists would realize a cost savings.

Stakeholder input

Stakeholders were not specifically asked about this proposal. In prior MEPRI studies, practitioners have pointed to a lack of funding for academic and behavioral specialists as an area of inadequacy in the EPS funding model.

Option 3

Regional Support for Effective Implementation of MTSS

This policy option would provide funding for an implementation coach for each superintendent region to help all districts to assess the quality of their MTSS systems, and to make improvements as needed to ensure the systems are implemented and working as intended. This would, over time, create more consistency and raise the quality of supports in districts that are currently lacking. Well-implemented MTSS systems are demonstrated to reduce the need for costly special education services, resulting in cost savings over time.

Considerations

MTSS has a large return on investment when done well. Implementation of this policy could be transformative.

The system requires alignment beginning with the classroom teachers’ practices, who require coaching to implement the model. The time and attention needed are beyond the capacity and expertise of a building principal and require coaching, especially during initial start-up while norms and procedures are established.

Cost implications

All schools are already expected to have Multi-Tiered Systems of Support (MTSS) in place to provide support to students who are not meeting academic (or behavioral) expectations. In theory, this is not a new cost. However, data collected by MDOE shows that implementation of MTSS varies widely. MDOE has some existing staff and already offers free professional development. However, there is no additional staff capacity to work with districts on assessing their needs and implementing the program. The addition of 9 staff to work more closely with struggling districts would cost roughly $1.5M per year. This means the initial investment would be expected to lead to longer-term net savings.

Stakeholder input

Recent MEPRI studies have demonstrated strong support from practitioners for additional support in implementing MTSS with fidelity to the model.

Topic area  ·  PK-8 Benchmarks + Curriculum

Accountability for Maine Learning Results

Ensure that all students are provided with opportunities to achieve the expectations outlined in the statewide standards.

Problem

Maine’s Learning Results and Guiding Principles establish the expectations that students should meet as part of a comprehensive public education. Schools do not have complete control over the academic performance of individual students; students themselves must put in the work. But schools are responsible for providing the breadth and depth of learning experiences that will enable students to achieve the standards. Reports from students and teachers indicate that this is not happening for all students. The Learning Results are currently being updated and hopefully streamlined, and these updates should make it more feasible for schools to offer a full range of programs and services. But better systems and more transparency is needed to verify that appropriate pathways are available to all students.

Option 1

Periodic Review of Academic Programs and Student Services

This policy option would require school districts to engage in periodic (e.g. every 5 years) peer review of their comprehensive education plans to obtain formative feedback from a team of practitioners who understand the local contexts and challenges. Any identified gaps in inputs that may be inhibiting students from achieving the Maine Learning Results would be identified and referred to MDOE’s Model for School Supports for remedy. This is distinct from the current school support model, which focuses only on schools with the most concerning student outcomes. An inputs-focused approach would ensure all schools are reviewed on a cyclical basis for the appropriateness of their curricula, staffing, and support programs and services.

Considerations

A revision of the Maine Learning Results is underway. A streamlining of the results could support a meaningful peer assessment centered on the essentials.

Cost implications

Districts are already expected to prepare and annually review their comprehensive education plans. The costs and staff time needed to peer review plans for others would be additional. However, a paper review of programs and services is substantially less involved than an accreditation review. Such peer conversations could be piggybacked on other convenings, thus avoiding travel costs. There may be a need for additional MDOE staff position(s) to review and analyze peer recommendations for areas needing attention. The MDOE school support model may or may not also need to expand; additional staff would be needed if more districts are to receive MDOE support.

Stakeholder input

In terms of review, we heard that it takes at least three years to implement a response to new standards, including a year of evaluation of new initiatives, and then the standards are up for review by MDOE, under the current system. A longer period of time under this option, such as every five years, gives time for teachers to develop, test, and iterate curriculum.

Some stakeholders advised that the MDOE could also provide this kind of review for districts, offering suggested targeted changes based on data already collected and a district’s comprehensive education plan.

Teachers generally feel that anchoring to the Maine Learning Results is a good idea. However, standards should be broad enough that teachers can be creative and innovative in meeting the learning needs of their students locally while adhering to what the state as a whole is prioritizing.

Policy Brief - Part Two

Below is a more detailed examination of topics sourced from the two top recommendations, which each earned 84% support in the second round of voting on Day Two.

Two Approaches

Strategic Approach

A multi-pronged, statewide, 10-year comprehensive strategy to advance Maine’s education system toward preparing students for successful, engaged adult lives of purpose. Such a strategy would center student engagement by prioritizing relevant, experiential learning. Elements could include developing a shared understanding of what it means to be prepared for life and a reflection of this in a streamlined Maine Learning Results; vertical alignment of agencies, committees, districts, and universities; deeper cross-sector partnerships; robust state and locally-designed accountability; and transparent, accessible channels for stakeholder voice in evolving policies and practices. States such as Rhode Island, Utah, North Dakota, Maryland, and Connecticut have in the past ten years created education visions and plans to direct, organize, and track progress. Maine could have a vision for true life-readiness that includes a foundation of experiential learning.

Led by our new governor, this approach could be transformative.

  • Create an initiative under the Governor’s Office of Policy Innovation and the Future for education transformation to coordinate with MDOE, DHHS, DOL and other appropriate stakeholders.
  • Develop an overall plan for vertical alignment of agencies, committees, and districts and cross-sector partnerships over 18 months to 2 years.
  • Propose a budget tied to current funding sources, with funding for the transition itself coming from new, named sources, if necessary.

Topic area  ·  Life skills and Experiential learning

Career and Life Ready Schools

The transformation of Maine’s education system toward career and life readiness has already begun, as the first option below reflects. However, major hurdles stand in the way of the rapid change young people are showing us we need. Secondarily, Maine’s schools are aging and in need of substantial investment just to stay open. And third, population shifts have forced the consolidation question, again. These two options won’t solve all of the problems Maine faces, but they show some promising directions to consider.

Pre-high school students need earlier exposure to post-secondary options for continued education, service, and careers in order to develop personal aspirations and make appropriate educational choices and plans in high school. When gradual and age-appropriate exposure, exploration, and experiences outside the school campus are incorporated into the PK-12 trajectory, school can feel more relevant and be more engaging for students of all ages. Students also need a high school education that provides the environment and opportunities for them to create their own pathways, building on those earlier experiences, and drive their own learning in preparation for successful launch into adulthood. These two needs are not mutually exclusive—the second option below is an expansion of the first and additionally addresses the school construction crisis and educator workforce needs.

Problem

Students too often feel disengaged from their education and unprepared to choose and traverse life pathways after high school, contributing to chronic absenteeism, a weaker workforce, and less desirable outcomes for young adults. School districts are not generally well resourced to provide the kinds of approaches that could help: student-centered, hands-on, experiential learning as the default or a PK-12 age-appropriate progression of experiences and knowledge that readies students for choosing their own path after high school.

Option 1

Expand the ASPIRE Initiative Statewide

The Aspire Initiative is a partnership among a local school district, JMG, and the MELMAC Education Foundation to support all students from early learning through high school graduation and into post-secondary experiences in ways that prepare them to successfully join the workforce. The successful partnerships demonstrate the impact of the ASPIRE model in embedding appropriate career-focused learning along the PK-12 continuum.

JMG, through the initiative, adds “capacity to help provide learning opportunities that focus on career exposure, exploration, and experience, and the development of future-ready skills. To do this:

  • Each school and teacher has the latitude to develop activities that work best for their students, staff, and families.
  • We learn from students and teachers about existing opportunities and how we can enhance those to help incorporate authentic career education into existing curriculum.
  • We provide additional human, financial, and community resources to design extended learning opportunities meaningful to teachers, their students, and our communities.
  • We share JMG, MELMAC, and other Aspire initiatives’ best practices to create learning communities to elevate all.
  • We collaborate and celebrate the amazing work to amplify and expand opportunities to more students, teachers, and community partners.”1

Many more schools already have a piece of the puzzle with existing JMG programs. With additional funding, these sites could augment their offerings with additional elements of the full ASPIRE model, and schools without current JMG programs could begin with those foundational pieces.

Considerations

When relevance to life is limited to later high school years, much of a student’s education can feel dry and even unimportant, contributing to absenteeism and low engagement. However, care must be taken not to focus on narrow career tracking, especially before high school. The emphasis is on broadening students’ perspectives and understanding of their options and the tradeoffs associated with different pathways. The Aspire model focuses on developmentally-appropriate exposure (elementary level) and exploration (middle level) before progressing to career experiences such as mentorships or internships in high school.

Schools receiving investment can actively share the model with other schools if they have “train the trainer” staff.

Cost implications

Maine currently provides about $4M to JMG to provide support to 13,000 students in 150 programs across the state. MELMAC has supplemented these programs with 5-year awards of over $1M to each of four school districts to transform their systems for career education. Additional districts could be funded at a similar cost (about $250k per district per year).

Stakeholder input

According to district leaders involved in an Aspire initiative, in order for this investment to be successful, a district needs to have certain assets already in place. Districts that are ready and able to implement can serve as first adopters, testing and refining the model to improve it for those who follow.

District leaders in ASPIRE schools also report that both students and teachers are engaged through the program, as teachers are enabled to think more expansively about how their courses connect to life and work, and students experience learning outside the classroom.

Option 2

Experiential Learning Lab High Schools

This policy option would expand the Integrated, Consolidated 9-16 Educational Facilities Program Funding opportunity2, which is in the second phase in the current application cycle. The expansion would not only direct funds to more schools, it would streamline the application process by directing the MDOE to partner with MSSA, the UMaine system, and any relevant outside organizations in creating a statewide assessment of assets and needs to inform a strategic approach to situating Beacon Schools.

This team would create a strategic plan for promoting and supporting the transformation of Maine’s high schools into experiential, career- and college-connected laboratories for learning. Experiential learning would be the foundational approach. The current program requires a partnership with a UMaine campus; this model deepens partnership with the UMaine system to create a pre-service teacher learning lab in each high school, ensuring regionally-relevant opportunities for pre-service educators to gain meaningful, real-world experiences and experienced educators to advance their practice.

These lab schools would also be designed as Beacon Schools, regional professional learning centers for preservice and certified administrators and teachers, igniting interest in and building capacity for experiential, real-world, career-connected learning in all schools.

Considerations

The XQ Institute3 is a valuable resource that could be leveraged in this program. Nearby, Rhode Island has partnered with the XQ Institute to reimagine its high schools for real-world, experiential learning as a part of a multi-year strategic approach.

This option is rated not quite a full “carrot” because districts wanting new buildings in areas that are ripe for this model may feel they need to consolidate if they want construction funding.

While there is indeed a lot of excitement from districts placed all over the state, not every district that might want to be a Beacon School has the assets needed or the necessary geographic placement. This option acknowledges those facts and moves forward with a plan that can indeed promote progress in all schools, even those not directly funded within this program. Asset mapping is a key aspect of the application process and criteria need to be clearly outlined so that districts don’t waste time applying for something they are not well positioned for. One approach would be to convene MSMA state leaders with MDOE to review asset maps, needs assessments, and geographic realities and create a shared understanding of where Beacon Schools could be built over a twenty-year period, perhaps supported by research staff. This would remove the burden from individual districts to enter multi-year application processes only not to be selected.

Cost implications

This program already exists, and has for almost a decade, without money yet being spent. While additional funding would surely be needed at some point, a strategic approach would seek to include replacing buildings already at the end of their lifespan, thereby addressing the crisis of school construction as well.

Investment in Beacon Schools can be understood to be an investment in a region, increasing teacher development opportunities, drawing new teachers, and providing a better prepared workforce and more community-connected citizens.

The cost of constructing an entirely new high school is high. The recently-completed Sanford High School, which has many features of a Beacon School, cost $103M in 2018. It may be possible to convert an existing school (or a CTE Region) to a Beacon School at a lower cost.

Stakeholder input

Maine School Board Association and Maine Curriculum Leaders members shared very similar views overall.

Concerns: Total cost, remote schools being left out, transportation issues, historic inability of districts to “get along,” deprioritization of the “dire school construction crisis,” a sense that it is unnecessary to transform in this way, and that it removes pressure from all schools to transform toward experiential learning.

Positives: Stronger regional network of opportunities for students, more career-connected experiential learning, more efficient use of resources for the desired outcomes, deeper community engagement with schools, stronger connection to community for students, potential to interest businesses in contributing to budgets, potential to improve chronic absenteeism, could scale well, could expand access to CTE-like experiences, proactively addresses declining populations, builds on existing excitement for 9-16 high school options, and builds on the successful model of CTE — which, like CTE, could provide a “learner-focused curriculum in a well-structured setting.”

Superintendents shared enthusiasm for growth of the ASPIRE model while incorporating the “Beacon School” concept. Concerns centered on the readiness of each school to participate. Fairness in selection of schools was also mentioned, with the advice to make site selection non-negotiable in order to avoid the historic adversity the consolidated 9-16 program has faced due to cross-district disagreements about siting.

Topic area  ·  Life skills

Life-Readiness for Maine’s Young Adults

Ensure all Maine young adults have the skills necessary for adult life. Life skills are defined here in this policy as the full set of life-readiness skills students need: self-knowledge and futures planning skills, careers knowledge, civic literacy, and adulting skills. This includes technical skills (e.g. vehicle and home maintenance and nutrition and cooking), civic literacy, durable human skills useful in personal and professional lives (e.g. collaboration, communication, self-knowledge, executive functioning), career options understanding, and foundational academic knowledge.

Problem

Maine’s young people are too often underprepared for the requirements of adult life. According to data from Maine ED 2050, news reporting, and anecdotal information, students themselves along with their parents, community members, and employers share concern that young adults are not ready for appropriate self-sufficiency, interdependency, or decision-making necessary for successful adult life. While some families and the schools their children attend do, together, provide most of what young adults may need, too many do not, due to a decreased focus on these topics in schools, hecticness of modern home life, intergenerational poverty, and/or a lack of this type of knowledge in older generations.

Option 1

Update the Definition of “Career and Life Ready” in the Maine Learning Results

This policy option would identify the full range of life-ready skills that are needed for self-sufficiency after high school, perhaps beginning with the definition provided above, with the exception of academic knowledge which is already in the Maine Learning Results. By including these skills in the list of things that students are expected to know and be able to do, the Learning Results will communicate the role that schools play in connecting students to rich learning opportunities that may not otherwise be available. (Note that some items may be already found in the Guiding Principles.)

Considerations

There is general agreement that streamlining the Maine Learning Results is necessary, therefore adding standards may be counterproductive. However, if the MLR can redefine Career and Life Ready to include topic areas (e.g. home economics, civic literacy, vehicle ownership and maintenance) rather than the discrete skills they comprise, that leaves room for local and regional organizations to develop rich, responsive programming community members are eager to teach. Too many “specs” would likely prevent non-professionals from bringing their skills and knowledge to young people.

To note is that this option alone does not provide any accountability for providing life skills opportunities, even if they are added to the Maine Learning Results. One option for tracking would be to explore a JMG-style badging system, which confers badges to students as they acquire specific skills within a framework.

Cost implications

The cost of expanding the Career and Life Ready standards is minimal.

Stakeholder input

Stakeholders – including parents, students, and employers – have expressed alarm at the lack of preparedness they see in some recent graduates. While some students have robust exposure to life-skill building through home, work or community involvement (e.g. scouting, Junior Achievement, etc.), others do not. Some stakeholders were reluctant to ask schools to take this on, and worried that a “curriculum” would be remedial or redundant for some students and work against the experiential nature of building life-readiness.

Option 2

Out-of-School Time Extended Learning Opportunities (ELOs) for Life-Readiness

This policy option would build on Maine’s extensive investment in Extended Learning Opportunities (ELOs) as a valid credit-bearing learning pathway by funding 12-month ELO coordinators in local districts and Regional ELO Coordinators at CTEs and encouraging cross-district engagement with communities and area employers. All districts would be eligible no matter their size. The local ELO coordinator would connect organizations and individuals to middle and high school students for extended learning opportunities in life-readiness skills and experiential learning connecting academics to life.

ELOs most often occur during the regular school year, but can also occur afterschool or during breaks or summer vacation. 12-month coordinator positions would ensure that afterschool and summer opportunities would be leveraged for students. Local districts approve credit for opportunities aligned with MLR life-readiness skills. District ELO coordinators would be supported by Regional ELO Coordinators employed at each CTE to ensure students benefit from opportunities across district lines. Funding for regional coordinators would come through the current CTE funding model.

Considerations

Whether CTEs are the best place for the Regional ELO Coordinators is an open question and needs stakeholder feedback. Regional Service Centers are another model that may also work well.

In theory, school districts that share a CTE are already expected to have coordinated school schedules. However, at times this has been problematic. In order to have a true regional ELO program that crosses district boundaries, superintendents will likely need to be engaged on supporting logistics.

Schools in this option would not be discouraged from providing opportunities for students to build expanded life-readiness skills, in addition to foundational academics, especially the durable human skills. This option simply supports additional pathways for students to gain these important skills all year long.

By promoting the learning of life-readiness skills in out-of-school time experiences, the question of teacher certification is circumvented.

Cost implications

Maine has provided about $7M in grants since 2022 to school districts to employ district-level ELO coordinators in 13 of 16 counties and 38 programs. This grant funding should be continued, and could even be included in the EPS model for an ongoing investment, to scale the program geographically and across grade levels as well as to include out-of-school time.

The CTE funding model also has an existing provision for each Center to hire a full-time student services coordinator; these positions are typically unfilled. This funding will need to be increased to ensure the staff members have capacity to add ELO coordination to their workload.

This option would also likely need to add funding for JMG to train and convene the Regional ELO Coordinators and expand its virtual programming, both of which would build on their current assets (and not take them in new directions).

Stakeholder input

Students in the Maine ED 2050 project made it clear: they need and want life-readiness skills, for themselves and their peers. They cite challenges with transportation and connections as barriers to gaining these skills outside of school, in addition to family lives too busy to teach them. Similarly, many parents in Maine ED 2050 saw school as the appropriate place, given their busy two-career family. Community members who fondly recall having “home ec” and “shop” classes see those as an essential part of a middle or high school education, especially for those students who are not learning these skills outside of school. This tension is resolved by placing a community-facing ELO Coordinator inside the school, where the students are, to facilitate a broader set of community interactions than may have been otherwise available for students.

Stakeholders by and large believe life skills are best taught in real-world settings, through experiential learning opportunities. Teachers, as noted above, have full plates already, and generally consider many of the life-readiness skills to be outside their purview.

Topic area  ·  Experiential learning

Experiential Learning

Expand developmentally-appropriate PK–12 experiential learning opportunities that connect academic learning to real-world experiences through professional development, curriculum, and community partnerships. Experiential learning is not a fad. It is an evidence-based approach to deeper learning that is widely used around the globe in PK-12, CTE, and adult learning. Major research outlets and national funders in education — Carnegie, for example — have been working hard to promote the experiential learning model as the next phase in the evolution of education and schooling. Simply put, people learn best when they are actively engaged in the learning process, whether they are contributing to a reflective discussion, collaborating on a solution to a community problem, or exploring chemistry in the lab.

Problem

Maine students are less engaged than they or their educators and parents would like. Low motivation toward learning was found in Maine ED 2050 to be one of the key obstacles to school success and personal thriving. When students are not engaged, they are less likely to come to school and less likely to come to class when they are at school. School has changed little in the past 100 years, in many ways, yet the world has changed a good deal. “Sit and get” learning approaches are not aligned with current brain science or educational best practices, nor do they excite and inspire students.

Option 1

Teacher Experiential Learning

This policy option would ensure teachers had experiential learning opportunities themselves, specifically in teaching using an experiential learning philosophy, as part of their initial teacher preparation. Teacher education programs would need to show how their students are trained in EL as part of their cyclical program approval reviews. Beginning teachers that do not complete a traditional preparation program with student teaching would need to demonstrate competency in EL as part of their district’s recommendation for progression from conditional to professional certification.

Considerations

Beginning teachers require practice and ongoing support to develop their craft; early-career teachers make substantial improvements in their first three years on the job. Experiential teaching may not develop right away among all the priorities being juggled. Continuing in-district support is needed.

This requirement may help keep some early-career teachers in the workforce as it aligns with teaching practices that many would prefer, but find hard to implement once on the job.

Cost implications

Existing teacher education programs often expose students to a variety of methods for engaging students in experiential, inquiry-based, project-based learning. These methods can be incorporated into existing courses as part of ongoing curriculum improvement. A more significant cost implication is the need for school districts that employ alternative-certification teachers – which is nearly all school districts – to have internal capacity to mentor their early-career staff in a full array of engaging teaching methods. Some schools currently lack adequate staff capacity and training for robust mentoring and will need to dedicate more attention to their induction programs.

Stakeholder input

Most stakeholders believe that beginning teachers need more practice with engaging teaching methods like EL as part of their initial preparation (among other foundational teaching skills). However, focusing only on traditional educator preparation pathways misses a large group of new teachers that pursue alternative pathways such as emergency certification and do not complete a guided student teaching experience. Both routes need to be considered to ensure that teachers universally receive this preparation. We did hear from the State Board of Education, which oversees teacher preparation requirements, that Rule Chapter 114 is planned for revisions in the near future.

Option 2

Teacher and Administrator Recertification Requirements

This policy option would require that all teachers, including early-career as well as experienced educators, engage in a district-provided experiential education learning opportunity themselves (as learners) at least once in the next five years. As part of the professional learning they would be asked to reflect on the implications for their own teaching practice, and (re)design curricula to incorporate more experiential learning opportunities embedded in academic content. Completion of this experience would be required for recertification.

Considerations

Adult learners also need time to engage in learning content in a hands-on way and have dedicated time to reflect on their practice. Unless teachers are exposed to high-quality experiential learning, it is hard to expect they will bring it into their own classrooms. Many schools are not employing these methods and so it cannot be assumed teachers are necessarily learning from their peers.

Such experiences do not necessarily need to be provided by the employing district – they could be offered by a university, a professional organization, or other non-profit – but it is reasonable to expect school districts to also walk the walk.

Cost implications

This does not add any new costs or additional contracted time. The required experience will count toward current recertification requirements, and not add to the total number of CEUs needed every 5 years.

Stakeholder input
